## PART ONE: THE LITURGY BENEATH

The hallucinatory text fragments the player encounters when throwing themselves off the train. These are not random — they form a coherent (but never fully comprehensible) description of a place, a process, and a congregation. The player pieces together fragments across multiple self-throws but never receives enough to explain anything.

### The Voice

The liturgical voice is not Christian, not Satanic, not any recognisable tradition. It sounds like all of them because it sounds like the *source* of all of them — testimony from a place that organised religion only half-remembers. The cadence is trinitarian (groups of three), the imagery is bodily, and the tone is that of someone describing a municipal process. Bureaucratic hell. Not fire and punishment — *procedure*.

### Visual Direction for the Hallucination

- **Colour:** A deep arterial red bleeds into the monochrome. Not bright — thick, dark, the colour of something that's been exposed to air. This is the ONLY colour in the entire game.
- **Text layout:** Words render at varying scales. Sermon fragments at large scale form the architecture — walls, ceiling, rows. Smaller text fills the space like texture, like grain in wood. The player is inside a room made of language.
- **Figures:** Silhouettes of seated people, built from densely packed small text. Rows deep. Hundreds of them. They don't move. They are the pews.
- **The pulpit/wound:** Centre of the composition. A shape that could be a lectern or a gash. Text radiates from it in all directions.
- **Blood:** The red doesn't overlay the text — it IS the background. The text is rendered in black on red, then in white on red, then just red on red (still faintly visible, like embossing). The space is made of blood the way the narrative says.

## PART TWO: THE PA ANNOUNCEMENT

A recurring ambient element. Plays at the start of each loop and occasionally mid-loop.

### The Announcement

A flat, automated voice. Gender-neutral. The cadence of a real transit announcement but the content is wrong.

**Standard (all loops):**
> "The next station is Acheron. Estimated arrival time is not available. Please remain in your seats."

The name "Acheron" is never explained, never commented on by passengers, never acknowledged as unusual. It's just the name.

**After high throw count (9+):**
> "The next station is Acheron. Estimated arrival time is not available. Please remain."

Shorter. The "in your seats" drops away. As if the instruction has eroded.

**After self-throw:**
> "The next station is Acheron. Estimated arrival time is not available. Welcome back."

The "welcome back" only appears once — the first time. After that, it's the standard announcement. But the player remembers it.

**Very late game (named passengers thinning):**
> "The next station is Acheron."

Just that. No instruction. No estimate.

**If only the player remains:**
> "Acheron."

## PART THREE: THE TRAIN

### Layout

Five accessible cars, plus the sealed car. The player starts in Car 3 (middle).

- **Car 1 (Front):** The Hellmouth Talker. A few unnamed passengers (followers, one panicker). The car closest to wherever the train is going. The windows here show the most "normal" landscape early on.
- **Car 2:** The Counter. The Liar. Several unnamed passengers. The most populated car early on. Tallies scratched into the wall near the Counter's seat.
- **Car 3 (Player's car):** The Mother. The Child. The Mute. The player's starting seat. The PA speaker is loudest here.
- **Car 4:** The Sleeper. A few unnamed passengers (still ones, a panicker). Quieter. The lights in this car occasionally flicker — not dramatically, just enough to notice.
- **Car 5 (Rear):** Empty seats. One or two unnamed passengers who never seem to be the same ones twice. The windows in this car show the landscape *behind* the train — where it's been. Except the landscape behind and ahead look the same.
- **The Sealed Car:** Between Car 5 and... nothing. A door that doesn't open. No window into it. The door has no handle on this side. It's not locked — there's simply no mechanism. Passengers who disappear are implied to be inside. The narration never confirms this.

### Movement

The player walks between cars. Each transition is described briefly:

> The door between cars is heavy. The gap between carriages is louder than you expect — wind, and something under the wind, a hum or a tone, not mechanical. You step across.

Later loops, this description shortens:

> You cross to Car 2.

Late game:

> Car 2.

The compression of traversal language mirrors the compression of everything else.

## PART FOUR: THE OPENING — LOOP ONE

### Scene: Waking

```
You are on a train.

You don't remember boarding. The seat beneath you is real — firm, 
slightly worn at the edges, the kind of upholstery that holds the 
shape of everyone who's sat in it. The window to your left shows 
dark fields. No moon. Power lines run alongside the track, leaning 
slightly, connecting to nothing visible.

The PA crackles.

"The next station is Acheron. Estimated arrival time is not 
available. Please remain in your seats."

The carriage is half-full. A woman across the aisle is settling a 
child — tucking a coat around small shoulders, murmuring something 
you can't hear. Further down, a figure sits facing the window. 
Still. You can't tell if they're awake.

The train moves. It has always been moving.
```

### Scene: First Exploration

The player can now move. Options:

- **Look around Car 3** — observes the Mother, the Child, the Mute, a few unnamed passengers
- **Go to Car 2** — meets the Counter, the Liar
- **Go to Car 4** — meets the Sleeper
- **Go to Car 1** — meets the Hellmouth Talker
- **Go to Car 5** — the rear car, mostly empty
- **Look out the window**
- **Stay in your seat**

### Car 3 — First Look

```
The woman with the child has finished settling them. She sits back. 
Her posture is the kind you learn — back straight, hands in lap, 
the practised stillness of someone used to waiting in places that 
don't move fast enough. She notices you looking.

"We'll get there."

She says it without conviction. Not lying — just providing the 
sound that the moment needs. The child is already asleep, or 
pretending to be.

The figure by the window hasn't moved. They're sitting with their 
hands on their knees, facing the glass. Their reflection should be 
visible but you can't quite resolve it. They might be looking at 
the landscape. They might be looking at nothing.

A man near the back of the car is talking to himself. No — he's 
talking to the woman next to him, but she's moved seats since he 
started. He hasn't noticed. He's describing something — a 
building, an interior, a room with very specific dimensions. 
Nobody is listening.

Three other passengers sit in scattered seats. One is crying 
quietly. One is staring at their phone — the screen is on but 
nothing is loading. One is gripping the armrest with both hands, 
knuckles white, as if the train might tip.
```

Wait — I've placed the Hellmouth Talker in Car 3 here, but he should be in Car 1. Let me correct the layout. The man talking in Car 3 would be a different unnamed passenger, or I should adjust. Let me keep the Talker in Car 1 as planned — that monologue-fragment in Car 3 can be an unnamed follower repeating something he heard from the Talker. A telephone-game distortion.

```
A man near the back of the car is murmuring. Not to anyone — to 
the air in front of him. You catch a phrase: "...the dimensions 
were specific, he said, very specific..." He's repeating something 
he heard. The words aren't his. His voice is flat with someone 
else's conviction.
```

**TIER 0 (Never thrown)**

*Player approaches. She looks up. The child stirs but doesn't wake.*

**Option A: "Are you alright?"**
> "We're fine. We're both fine."
> 
> She says it like a gate closing. Not rude — complete. You've been 
> given your answer and the answer is a wall. The child's hand 
> tightens on her sleeve.
> 
> "Have you tried the front car? Someone up there seems to know 
> things. Or thinks he does."

**Option B: "How long have you been on this train?"**
> She considers this.
> 
> "How long have you?"
> 
> You don't have an answer. She nods as if you've confirmed something.
> 
> "The child fell asleep twenty minutes ago. Or an hour. The light 
> hasn't changed. I've stopped using the light."

**Option C: "Do you know what's happening?"**
> "The train hasn't stopped. That's what's happening."
> 
> She looks at the window. The landscape is dark and featureless.
> 
> "I've been on delayed trains before. This isn't a delay. A delay 
> implies an arrival."
>

>

*She's at the door. She's already standing. When you enter the car she takes a step toward the door. Her hand is on the handle.*

No dialogue options. The narration:

```
She's there. She's always there now. The door, the handle,

The child sits in the seat. The follower-woman beside the child
```

### The Hellmouth Talker — Car 1

**TIER 0 (Never thrown)**

*He's mid-sentence when you enter the car. He has been mid-sentence since before you boarded. He doesn't acknowledge your arrival — you're walking into the middle of something.*

```
"...and the ceiling was not high. The ceiling was the exact 
height of a man standing with his arms raised, which is to say 
the ceiling was designed, which is to say someone measured a man 
and used that measurement, and the man was not alive when they 
measured him, I think, because a living man would lower his arms, 
but this ceiling — this ceiling assumed the arms stayed up, which 
they do, after, if you arrange them, which someone did..."
```

**Option A: "What are you talking about?"**
> He looks at you. First time he's focused on anyone.
> 
> "The room. I'm talking about the room."
> 
> As if you should know. As if everyone knows.
> 
> "The room with the low ceiling and the rows and the thing 
> at the front that speaks. I'm describing it. Someone should 
> be describing it. If no one describes it then it's just — 
> there. Without a record. And a thing without a record isn't 
> accountable."
> 
> He turns back to the empty seat beside him and continues.
> 
> "...the walls were red but not painted red, red in the way 
> that a thing is red when the redness is the material, not 
> the surface..."

**Option B: "Have you been there? The place you're describing?"**
> "Been there."
> 
> He tastes the word.
> 
> "Been is interesting. 'Been' implies leaving. I'm describing 
> it, aren't I? Does a man who's left need to describe the place 
> he's left? Or does he describe it because he hasn't?"
> 
> He taps the seat.
> 
> "I'm being precise. That's the important thing. The 
> dimensions. The material. The arrangement. If I'm precise 
> enough, the description becomes proof. And proof is — proof 
> is the opposite of faith. And faith is what the room runs on."

**Option C: "You sound insane."**
> "Yes. That's very useful, isn't it? For you. To decide 
> that. Makes the train simpler."
> 
> He smiles. It's a real smile. Warm, even.
> 
> "The insane man in Car 1, talking about a room. Easy to 
> dismiss. Easy to throw off a train, if it comes to that."
> 
> He knows. He doesn't know how he knows, but he knows.
> 
> "The room doesn't care if you believe in it. The room 
> is not a matter of belief. The room is a matter of 
> architecture."

### The Counter — Car 2

**TIER 0 (Never thrown)**

*He's in a window seat. The wall beside him has scratches — tidy groups of five, tallied, with annotations you can't read from here. He's writing in the condensation on the window with his fingertip when you approach.*

**Option A: "What are you counting?"**
> "Resets. Events. Movements."
> 
> He says it like you've asked what language he speaks.
> 
> "Every time the train does its thing — resets, loops, 
> whatever word you want — I mark it. I note who was where. 
> Who moved. What changed."
> 
> He gestures at the tallies.
> 
> "Forty-seven resets. Seat distribution follows a pattern. 
> Not predictable exactly, but bounded. There are rules."
> 
> He looks at you with the intensity of someone who needs 
> you to understand.
> 
> "There are rules. That means there's a system. That 
> means there's a way through."

**Option B: "Have you found anything?"**
> "Patterns. Several."
> 
> He pulls you closer to the wall, points at scratch-groups.
> 
> "The unnamed ones — the ones who don't talk, the ones 
> who panic — they shift. Not randomly. There's a drift. 
> Toward the back. Toward Car 5. Every three to four resets, 
> one of them moves a car further back."
> 
> His finger traces the tallies.
> 
> "And then they're gone. Not immediately — they sit in 
> Car 5 for a reset or two, then they're not there. They 
> don't come back."
> 
> He pauses.
> 
> "The sealed car. They go to the sealed car. I can't 
> prove it. But the numbers work."

**Option C: "This seems pointless."**
> He flinches. Actually flinches.
> 
> "It's not pointless. It's the only thing that isn't 
> pointless. Everything else on this train — the talking, 
> the sitting, the waiting — that's pointless. This — "
> 
> He touches the tallies.
> 
> "This is evidence. This is someone paying attention. If 
> there's ever an after — if someone finds this train, if 
> someone opens the sealed car, if something ends — this 
> is proof that someone was trying to understand."
> 
> He turns back to the window. Writes another mark in 
> the condensation. It fades before he lifts his finger.

### First Self-Throw

```

could be a seat or could be nothing, and the windows

and the train does not end.

Below you: not the ground. 

A colour. Red. Deep, arterial, the red of the 
inside of the body. Not a sky. Not a field. A 
surface that breathes. And on it — or in it —

The reset.

Welcome back.

didn't hear the difference. Or they've always 
heard it.
```

## PART SEVEN: THE WINDOW

### What the Player Sees Looking Out

The window is always available. A quiet action. No dialogue, no consequence — just observation. But what's outside changes.

**Early loops (0-3 resets):**
```
Dark fields. A sky without stars, without moon — 
not cloudy, just empty, as if the sky is a surface 
with nothing on it. Power lines run alongside the 
track, leaning slightly, their wires catching no 
light.

In the distance, structures. Not buildings — shapes. 
Rectangular, regular, the kind of shape that suggests 
human construction without confirming it. They don't 
get closer. They don't recede. They pace the train.

Your reflection in the glass is clear. Behind you, 
the car. The passengers. You can count them in the 
reflection.

The count is correct.
```

**Mid loops (4-7 resets):**
```
The fields are the same field. You've seen this 
stretch of nothing before — the same lean in the 
power lines, the same absence where the horizon 
should be. The landscape is repeating. Not looping 
the way the train loops — stuttering. The same 
frame held too long.

A tree passes. You've seen it before. The same tree. 
The same angle. It passes again.

Your reflection: the car behind you. The passengers. 
Fewer. You count them in the glass.

One more than there should be.

You turn around. The car is correct. The right 
number. You turn back to the window.

The extra reflection is gone. Or it was never there. 
Or it's seated behind someone, hidden, and you 
can't tell because reflections don't have depth, 
they just have surface, and the surface is —

The tree passes again.
```

**Late loops (8-11 resets):**
```
The landscape is wrong.

Not absent or repeating — wrong. The field is made 
of something that isn't earth. It's flat, textured, 
the consistency of — fabric? Skin? A surface that 
has pores. The power lines are gone. The structures 
in the distance are closer, and they are not 
buildings. They are rectangular, yes. Regular, yes. 
But they are the size of buildings and the shape of 
seats. Train seats. Standing upright in the field, 
rows deep, stretching to where the horizon should 
be.

A field of seats. Empty. Waiting.

Your reflection in the glass is wrong. It's you — 
your posture, your clothes, the angle of your head. 
But it's not moving when you move. It's looking at 
something you're not looking at. Its mouth is open 
slightly, as if it was saying something when you 
interrupted it by looking.

You look away.

When you look back, the reflection is correct.

The seats in the field are gone. The field is a 
field. The sky is empty.

Everything is fine.
```

**After self-throw:**
```
The landscape. You look.

For a moment — a frame, a flicker — the landscape 
is red. The field, the sky, all of it, the colour 
you saw when you fell. And in the red, just for 
that frame: the rows. The seated figures. The wound 
at the front, speaking.

Then it's gone. Dark fields. Empty sky. Power lines.

You saw it. The narration doesn't mention it.
```

*[Note: This last moment is where the narration and the visual layer disagree. The canvas shows the red flash. The text describes normal landscape. The game gaslights the player.]*

**Very late game (most unnamed passengers gone):**
```
Another train.

On a parallel track, running at the same speed, 
in the same direction. Same model. Same dark 
windows. Same number of cars.

You look at its windows.

You don't look at its windows.

You look at its windows.

In them, reflected or real or imagined: movement. 
People. The same configuration — someone standing, 
someone sitting, someone by the door.

The other train is this train. Or this train is 
the other train. Or there are many trains, parallel, 
synchronised, each one a car on a larger train, 
each one a seat in a longer row, each one a word 
in a longer sermon.

The other train passes. Or you pass it. Or neither 
of you are moving.
```

## PART EIGHT: THE DO-NOTHING PATH

If the player never throws anyone, never throws themselves, and simply rides the train — sitting, looking, walking between cars, talking but never acting — the game responds.

### Phase 1 — Loops 1-3 (The Train Waits)

Normal play. The narration is present, detailed, second-person. "You walk to Car 2. The Counter looks up." The game doesn't punish inaction — it describes the world in full.

### Phase 2 — Loops 4-6 (The Train Notices)

```
The narration begins to comment on your stillness.

"You're still here. Same seat. The train has reset 
three times since you last stood up."

Other passengers glance at you. Not with fear — 
with something else. Recognition. The panickers 
panic less around you. The followers drift toward 
your car. The child sits closer.

You are becoming a fixed point. The thing that 
doesn't move. On a train where everything resets, 
the person who does nothing is the most unusual 
thing happening.
```

### Phase 3 — Loops 7-9 (The Train Adjusts)

```
The narration starts to change perspective. Subtly.

"The man in Car 3 has not moved. He sits in his 
seat. The landscape passes."

Third person. Brief. You're being described the 
way the unnamed passengers are described — as 
furniture, as context, as part of the car's 
composition.

Other passengers stop approaching you. The Mother 
glances at you with something that isn't concern — 
it's the look she gives the still ones. The ones 
who are running down.

"The still one in Car 3. He sits. The train moves."
```

### Phase 4 — Loops 10-12 (The Train Absorbs)

```
"Car 3. A seat is occupied. The window shows dark 
fields."

No "you." No name. No interiority. The narration 
describes the car and you are in it the way a seat 
is in it. Object, not subject.

The Sleeper says something from Car 4. You can't 
hear it because the narration isn't reporting your 
senses anymore. It's reporting the train's.

The Mute sits beside you. This is noted:

"The quiet one and the still one. Car 3. Adjacent."

You can still act. The game still responds to 
input. But the narration treats your actions like 
weather:

"The still one stands. Crosses to the window. Sits 
again. Car 3 is unchanged."
```

### Phase 5 — The Seat

```
This is the final state. The narration is one or 
two lines per loop. The perspective is that of 
the train itself — an inventory.

"Car 1: the talker, two others. Car 2: the counter, 
one other. Car 3: the mother, the child, the quiet 
one, the seated one. Car 4: the sleeper. Car 5: 
empty. Sealed car: [no report]."

You are "the seated one." You have been absorbed 
into the train's taxonomy. You are a feature.

The final line:

"The next station is Acheron. The seated one 
does not look up. The train continues."

[The game does not end. It loops. The inventory 
repeats. Each loop, the narration is identical. 
The player can still act — stand, move, talk — 
but if they do, they break the state and the game 
re-engages in second person, almost reluctantly, 
as if waking them from something.]

[If they don't act for three consecutive loops, 
the screen slowly fades to black. Not a game 
over. Not a conclusion. The text on a black screen:]

"The seat holds the shape of everyone who's sat 
in it."

[Nothing else. The game is over. This is the 
quietest ending in the series.]
```

## PART NINE: THE SEALED CAR

The sealed car is never entered. The sealed car is never described from the inside. The sealed car is the absence around which the rest of the game organises.

### What the Player Can Do

- **Approach the door:** "The door at the end of Car 5. No handle. No window. The surface is the same as every other door on the train — grey, metal, a fire safety notice that you can't quite read. The only difference is the absence. No way to open it. Not locked. Not jammed. Simply not designed to be opened from this side."
- **Touch the door:** "It's warm. Not hot — warm. Body temperature. The temperature of something alive, or something that contains something alive, or something that was alive recently enough that the warmth hasn't left."
- **Listen at the door:** "Nothing. You press your ear to the metal. Nothing. But the nothing is thick — the nothing of a room with held breath, not the nothing of a room that's empty. Pressurised silence. The silence of very still people."
- **Knock:** "You knock. The sound doesn't carry the way it should. It deadens immediately, as if the door absorbed it. As if the door was hungry for sound and took it before it could echo."

### What Happens Around It

The player never sees anyone enter. But the population count drops. The narration handles this through absence:

**A panicker who was in Car 2 last loop is gone.** The narration doesn't say "the panicker is gone." It describes Car 2 and the panicker isn't in the description. The absence is something the player has to notice.

**The followers thin.** Three become two. Two become one. The last follower sits in Car 5, alone, facing the sealed door. Next loop: Car 5 is empty.

**Eventually:** The still ones go. They were panickers who stopped panicking. They sat still. They drifted back through the cars. They sat in Car 5. They're not there anymore.

The sealed car never fills up. The sealed car never overflows. The sealed car never makes a sound. The sealed car is patient.

### The Implication

The sealed car is where you go when you stop. When you stop trying, stop panicking, stop being a person who acts and become a person who sits. The still ones went there. The followers went there. The unnamed went there.

The named passengers don't go. The named passengers are still *doing something* — talking, counting, lying, sleeping, not-speaking. Even the Mute is doing something: sitting with purpose, with orientation, with meaning. The unnamed passengers run out of purpose. The sealed car takes them.

The player, if they choose the do-nothing path, approaches this state but never crosses. The narration absorbs them, the taxonomy recategorises them, but they never go to the sealed car. They sit on the threshold of it. The seat holds their shape. The game ends before the door opens.

Because the game is kind. Or because the game is the door. And it was never sealed from the outside.

## PART TEN: THE RESET

How the reset feels, mechanically and narratively.

### Early Resets (Loops 1-3)

```
The train lurches. Not a normal lurch — a 
grammatical one, as if the sentence you were in 
ended before the thought finished. A cut. A splice.

The PA crackles. "The next station is Acheron."

You're in your seat. You were standing. Now you're 
in your seat. The transition had no duration. It's 
not that you don't remember sitting down — sitting 
down didn't happen. You were standing and now the 
standing is over and you're in a new sentence.

The passengers are in their seats. The Mother is 
settling the child. The man near the back is 
murmuring about dimensions. The figure by the 
window is still.

Everything is the same. Everything is exactly, 
precisely, meticulously the same.

Except you remember. And from the way the Mother 
isn't looking at you — the specific, practised way 
she's not looking at you — she remembers too.
```

### Mid Resets (Loops 4-7)

```
Reset.

You're in your seat. Your body knows the lurch now — 
anticipates it, or remembers it, or never stopped 
feeling the last one. The transitions are getting 
heavier. Not longer. Heavier. There's a weight to 
them, a physical sense of being picked up and put 
down, like the train is clearing its throat.

Fewer people. The car is less full. You don't count. 
You already know.

The Hellmouth Talker is talking. Further into the 
monologue. The Counter is at his wall. The Mother is 
settling the child. Everything is the same.

Except the PA is slightly louder. Or slightly closer. 
Or the car is slightly smaller. Or you are slightly 
less than you were.

"The next station is Acheron. Please remain."

Please remain.
```

### Late Resets (Loops 8+)

```
Reset.

Seat. Hands. Window. Acheron.

The reset is a single beat now. No lurch, no 
transition. A blink. You were in a loop and now 
you're in a loop and the loops have gotten so 
close together that the space between them is 
just a seam, a stitch, a hyphen between two 
instances of the same word.

The car is quiet. Most of the unnamed are gone. 
The named passengers are in their positions — 
wherever their throw count has put them. Some 
by the door. Some in their seats. The Mute is 
near you. The Mute is always near you now.

The landscape outside is the same landscape. The 
same dark. The same lean in the power lines that 
aren't there anymore.

"Acheron."

The train continues. It has always been continuing.
```

## PART ELEVEN: TITLE CARD AND OPENING

### Before Loop 1

The game opens on black. Nothing for three seconds. Then:

```
TERMINUS
```

White text on black. Holds for four seconds. Then:

```
The next station is Acheron.
```

Smaller. Below. Holds for three seconds. Then black again.

Then the opening scene: "You are on a train."

### Closing

There is no closing. The game loops. The game has always been looping. The endings (the do-nothing fade, the self-throw dissolution, the continued throwing) are states, not conclusions. The player closes the browser tab. That's the ending.

The train continues.

## APPENDIX: NARRATIVE RULES

1. **Never explain the train.** Never explain the loop. Never explain the sealed car. Never explain the red place. The moment you explain, it becomes a problem with a solution. It is not a problem. It is a condition.

2. **The narration is complicit.** It describes what happens. It does not judge. It does not comment on cruelty. It does not reassure. It is the voice of the train.

3. **Horror through texture, not event.** Nothing jumps out. Nothing attacks. The horror is the warmth of the sealed car door. The horror is the child not going to the Mother. The horror is the Sleeper opening her eyes. The horror is small and domestic and worse for it.

4. **The player is the most violent thing on the train.** There is no monster. There is no entity. There is the player, choosing to throw people through a door. The red place exists, the sermon exists, the wound exists — but they don't act on the passengers. The player does.

5. **Silence is louder than speech.** The Mute says more than the Talker. The do-nothing path is more devastating than any throw. The sealed car's silence is fuller than the sermon. Restraint is the primary instrument.

6. **The only colour is blood.** Monochrome except for the self-throw hallucination. The red is earned. It appears once, maybe twice. It is the only moment the game breaks its own visual rules, and it should feel like a wound in the screen.

7. **No fourth wall breaks.** The game never acknowledges that it's a game. The narration never addresses the player as a player. The choices are presented as actions, not mechanics. The horror is that it feels real because the narration treats it as real.

8. **The Hellmouth Talker is always right.** Everything he describes is accurate. The player will never confirm this within the game, but the internal consistency should be absolute. His monologue and the sermon fragments describe the same place. He is the most reliable narrator on the train. He sounds the least reliable. That's the point.
